Geo Digest · 2026-W35 · Aug 24–30, 2026

America can no longer make other countries do what it wants without their agreement.

So this week its allies, the countries that control key sea routes, and the people who lend it money all started charging it a price. Canada answered America's 50% import taxes with import taxes of its own. Iran set a price for letting ships through the , the narrow sea passage that carries much of the world's oil. Japan spent a record $96.5bn defending its currency and still failed. Three bills, one cause.

First, the rule that never changes

The rule

Q: Who pays to keep the world's shipping lanes open and its alliances working?

A: For 80 years, the United States. It paid for the navy, the alliances and the rules, and everyone else used them for free.

Q: What happens when it can no longer afford that?

A: Nothing gets announced. Whoever physically controls something simply starts charging for it.

Q: Who started charging this week?

A: Iran, which controls a sea passage. Canada, which controls a border. And the people who lend America money.

Think of it like this. A landlord used to pay for the water, the heating and the security guard in his building. Then he runs out of money. He does not tell the tenants. So the water company starts billing them directly. So does the heating company. So does the guard. Each bill looks small. Together they cost more than the landlord ever paid.

The landlord is the United States. This week the tenants got three separate bills.

The whole week as one picture

The weakest link, and what the picture shows. The arrow from Asian borrowing costs to Vietnam is the weak one. No source wrote this week that costlier borrowing across Asia raises what Vietnam pays to borrow. So that arrow rests on how the system works, not on anything reported. Watch what Vietnam is charged the next time its government or a state-owned company borrows.

The rest of the picture shows one thing. Every branch starts at the same place, a country that can no longer enforce what it promises. And every branch ends with somebody else collecting a fee.

Now, step by step

Vietnam

The mechanism

Q: What is the difference between renting a thing and owning it?

A: If you rent a van, the owner can raise the price or refuse you next month. If you buy it, nobody can do either.

Q: Why does that matter to a country?

A: Weapons work the same way. Every one a country builds itself is one thing a foreign government cannot take away or put a price on.

Think of it like this. Two builders do the same job. One rents his tools by the week, the other owns his. In a good year they earn the same. In a bad year, only one of them can still work.

  1. A Vietnamese shipyard, Sông Thu, started building the country's largest warship. It weighs 2,000–3,000 tonnes and is built to hunt submarines. Work began in Da Nang on 10 August.becausethis ends ten years in which Vietnam ordered no warship of that size. Fulcrum, Aug 28
  2. So why build it at home, and why now? Because the supplier Vietnam relied on for decades has stopped delivering. Russia armed it for years and no longer fills the orders.becausea supply line can fail without anybody announcing it. By the time the delivery does not arrive, it is years too late to start building your own.
  3. So Vietnam is answering the question of who will defend it with equipment it owns. The ship carries a radar Vietnam designed itself and 12 missile tubes.becauseno foreign leader can cancel something a country builds at home. That is what self-reliance means in practice.
  4. And Indonesia reached the same answer by the opposite road. Its foreign and defence ministers met China's and agreed to build ammunition, rocket and missile factories in Indonesia.becausewhoever builds the factory sets the terms. Indonesia got the factories and paid by opening its defence industry to China. The Diplomat, Aug 25
  5. But owning weapons does not buy the right to sell your goods abroad. Indonesia's trade deal with America makes it copy American import bans on other countries. It must also ask Washington before picking suppliers for phone networks and undersea cables.becauseFulcrum reads that as a way of boxing China in, written into a trade deal instead of a defence pact. Vietnam's deal will copy it. Fulcrum, Aug 27
  6. And a shipyard cannot fix the other gap, which is workers. About 65% of Vietnamese people work with no contract and no benefits. So Vietnam is sending workers abroad and courting skilled Vietnamese overseas at the same time.becauseVietnam Weekly calls that two-sided bet a forced one. Workers, not money, are now what limits growth. Vietnam Weekly, Aug 25
What most people missMost readers will treat the warship as a story about the South China Sea. It is a story about suppliers. Vietnam found that a supply line can stop working without anybody announcing it, and South Korea and Indonesia made the same discovery about America this week. A second miss cuts against the good news. Fulcrum argues that Vietnamese people learned to trade and save under scarcity long before the 1986 reforms. Fulcrum, Aug 26 If habits drove the growth, no policy can switch it back on.

Switzerland

The mechanism

Q: What does it cost to stay out of everyone else's fight?

A: More than saying you are out of it. Neutrality is a bill, paid in money, weapons and law.

Q: Why does that bill rise when the world gets rougher?

A: Because a neutral country has no ally obliged to come and help. Whatever it needs, it has to buy for itself.

Think of it like this. A house on a quiet street needs no locks. Then the street stops being quiet. Saying "this is not my quarrel" changes nothing. Buying a solid door, an alarm and insurance does. The neighbours who never had a quiet street bought theirs years ago.

  1. Christoph Blocher put more than CHF 2m of his own money into the campaign for the neutrality vote. The Yes side now has twice the budget of the No side, three weeks before the vote on 27 September.becausegovernment audit records showed the funding gap. If the vote passes, neutrality goes into the Swiss constitution. NZZ, Aug 28
  2. So why write it into the constitution at all, and why now? Because supporters believe the government has already drifted away from neutrality, and that the pull to pick a side is getting stronger every month.becauseformer diplomat Paul Widmer puts the case at its most respectable. Real neutrality demands steadfastness, and no minister is allowed to sit down with a country at war. NZZ, Aug 26
  3. And a constitutional rule is the only kind a future government cannot quietly ignore. An ordinary policy can be reversed by the next cabinet.becausethat is the whole point of the timing. Once a country has taken a side, no later vote takes it back.
  4. So campaign money, at two to one, now decides whether Switzerland keeps any freedom to act.becausethe whole case against the vote is that a constitutional lock removes that freedom. Freedom to act is exactly what is scarce now. NZZ, Aug 26
  5. And the advice from outside says declaring neutrality buys nothing. Poland's ambassador Marek Prawda told the Swiss «Es gibt keinen Frieden durch Erzählungen, sondern nur durch effektive Abschreckung» ("there is no peace through narratives, only through effective deterrence").becausea country on Russia's border tells a neutral one that only real weapons stop an attack. NZZ, Aug 29
  6. But the money for those weapons just got smaller and slower. Finance politicians in the upper house of parliament want a smaller army tax rise, spread over more years. If the tax vote fails, the shortfall hits the general budget and not the army.becauserearming is now a fight about tax, not about security. The authors added that fallback because they expect to lose the vote. NZZ, Aug 25
  7. So the only thing Switzerland bought this week was cheap. Defence minister Pfister plans one centre for military and civilian drones and robots. The site, the budget and the legal basis are all still open.becausethis purchase needs no new tax and no public vote. It gathers scattered anti-drone work into one place. NZZ, Aug 27
What most people missSwitzerland argues about neutrality as identity and then settles it with money. Meanwhile the franc, the one thing Bern never has to legislate, is set abroad. Central bank board member Petra Tschudin explains it simply. Foreign oil prices raise inflation abroad, so foreign interest rates rise, so money leaves the franc. She keeps the Swiss rate at 0%, and on going below zero says «dann werden wir das auch tun» ("then we will do that too"). SNB, Aug 24 That is the Strait of Hormuz arriving in a Swiss savings account.

United States

The mechanism

Q: When does a threat stop being a threat?

A: The moment you need the other side's permission to carry it out.

Q: What does it turn into instead?

A: A request. And both sides can see that is what happened.

Think of it like this. A shop owner tells a customer: pay up, or I bar you from the shop. That works — unless the customer is also his only supplier. Then barring him closes the shop. Both of them know it.

America is the shop owner this week. China is the supplier.

  1. America announced "Operation Economic Outcast". It punishes any country, bank or company that does business with Iran, not only Iran itself. China called it illegal the same day.becauseChina buys more than 80% of Iran's oil. It also processes most of the world's rare-earth metals, which go into almost every electronic device. BBC, Aug 25
  2. So why do it now? Because America's midterm elections are 67 days away, and fuel prices are the one thing every voter notices.becausethe BBC reports that Iran is betting on exactly that. Tehran expects higher American petrol prices to become a political problem before November. BBC, Aug 25
  3. Which makes the announcement a bluff. To make it real, America must punish Chinese banks. It will not touch them.becauseO'Brien reads America's own answer as an admission. Treasury Secretary Bessent asked: "Why would I want to blow up the global financial system?" Phillips P. O'Brien, Aug 26
  4. So America took the physical thing instead. A new jointly owned company gives it 55% and a 100-year right to 17 Venezuelan oil fields holding 65bn barrels.becausethe BBC notes this goes further than America's control of Iraqi oil money in 2003. No official text has been published. BBC, Aug 29
  5. And the tool that still works landed on a friend, not an enemy. Import taxes of 50% hit about $20bn of Canadian goods. Canada is matching them from 8 September, aiming its list at states where Republican senators face close races.becausea friend is the one country too tied in to leave. Setser reads Canada's refusal as an example the EU is now watching. Geopolitical Futures, Aug 28
  6. And America pays for all of it by borrowing for 10 years or more, and too few people want to lend that long. Warsh says the central bank still has "work to do". Four of America's regional central banks asked for 4% in July.becausethose banks blame import taxes and Hormuz fuel prices. So America's own pressure is raising what it pays to borrow. Federal Reserve, Aug 25
What most people missReaders will read the Venezuela deal as strength and the sanctions as escalation. Both are second choices, reached for because the first choice needs a rival's agreement. The quieter evidence is one budget line. Four sources tell the BBC that the Pentagon will let its $61m agreement with the Kurdish Peshmerga expire on 30 September, covering about 180,000 troops. The last coalition troops leave Iraq the same day. BBC, Aug 28 A country that seizes 65bn barrels while dropping a $61m partner is not choosing priorities. It is running out of them.

China

The mechanism

Q: How do you win an argument without joining it?

A: Be the thing the other side cannot act without.

Q: Why is that stronger than threatening back?

A: A threat can be answered. A dependency cannot. It costs nothing to hold and never has to be used.

Think of it like this. One supplier on the street makes the part every factory needs. He never threatens anybody. He does not have to. Nothing gets built unless he agrees, so everyone quietly works around him.

  1. The new sanctions named every other country, bank and company. They left China's purchases of Iranian oil alone.becauseO'Brien argues that sanctions which exclude the buyer that matters show weakness, not pressure. Phillips P. O'Brien, Aug 26
  2. So China gained the power to stop America's financial weapon, and paid nothing to get it.becausethe exemption is built in. It comes from the world's need for Chinese banks and Chinese rare-earth metals, not from anything Beijing had to negotiate. BBC, Aug 25
  3. So China spent where America had just left, and it spent now for that reason. Its foreign and defence ministers met Indonesia's and agreed to more joint exercises, plus ammunition, rocket and missile factories in Indonesia.becausethe moment to offer a country a factory is the month it stops trusting its old protector. A warship exercise east of Taiwan on 12 August came first. Geopolitical Futures, Aug 28
  4. But the bill for that has nowhere left to go. Pettis shows that ordinary families paid for China's last bank rescue in the early 2000s. Their savings earned less than inflation, moving about 5% of the economy a year to the banks.becausefamilies cannot pay on that scale again. So the next rescue falls on companies or the state, and each is a separate political fight. Carnegie, Aug 25
  5. So the strain now shows in people, not in paper. Youth unemployment reached 17.9% in July, with 12.7m graduates entering a crowded job market. Beijing then gave injury insurance to 84m delivery riders and other app workers.becauseprotection is replacing the growth that used to lift people into the middle class. That is a sign of strain at home, not a welfare milestone. East Asia Forum, Aug 29
What most people missChina's week looks like a run of free wins. Each one is paid for somewhere the reader cannot see. The escape valve is exports. Carmaker BYD's profit recovered because sales abroad made up for the share it lost at home. Nikkei reports that Beijing itself now worries that exporting its price war invites other countries to shut it out. Nikkei Asia, Aug 28 And it lands on exactly the factory work Vietnam is trying to move into.

Seen from their own capital

  • Ottawa — it wants to prove that refusing works. Canada matched America's 50% taxes and aimed its list at states where Republican senators face close races. The raw numbers favour America, so the bet is political: other allies are watching whether refusing works.
  • Seoul — it wants a defence that no foreign election can cancel. It holds leverage it has never used, in parts for American warships and in memory chips. What it cannot do is wait: building its own nuclear weapon before the American promise lapses is insurance, and building it after looks like an attack on the alliance.
  • Tehran — it wants the government to survive, and the toll gate is the last thing that still works for it. But Gulf states are already shipping oil out the expensive way, so the gate is worth less every month.
  • Jakarta — it wants the ability to make things rather than a side, and both sides will sell to it. Taking no side used to be a stance. It is now a shopping list.

What most people miss

The part most people miss

A problem almost never appears where it was caused. This week the gap is widest in the least dramatic story. Japan's ¥15.39tn spend on its own currency looks like a Japanese problem. It started in the Strait of Hormuz. Oil through the strait fell to about 4.9 million barrels a day from 21.6 million two quarters earlier. That made fuel a permanent part of what things cost in rich countries. That is why Warsh talks about raising rates. And that is why the gap between American and Japanese rates ate the largest currency defence in history in four weeks.

Switzerland's weak franc is the same chain with a different ending, and the Swiss central bank says so itself. Neither has anything to do with bad decisions in Tokyo or Bern.

The second point matters more. Sanctions that let off the one country that matters are not weaker sanctions. They are a different thing entirely, and they teach that country exactly what it is worth. Every government watching this week learned where the limit of American pressure lies. That is why the response is factories and not diplomacy. South Korea moves toward its own deterrent. Indonesia moves toward Chinese factories. Vietnam moves toward its own warship. None of those answer a policy. They answer a demonstration of where the policy stops.

Hold on to one imbalance. The tolls are collected in cash. The enforcement is paid for with borrowed money. Iran wants $0.10–$2 a barrel and needs no capital to charge it. Somali pirates have attacked thirteen ships since January, the worst run in more than a decade. Canada's answer cost its prime minister a signature. Against that, America borrows for 10 years or more to pay for every tool it used this week. That covers the sanctions machinery, the carrier movements and the Venezuelan oil rights. Four of its own regional central banks want that borrowing to cost more, and Japan can no longer be counted on to lend. This is not a contest of will. It is a contest of running costs, and America is on the expensive side of it.

So what would change the answer

Everything on this page rests on one step: whether any country actually collects a published fee for letting ships pass. Iran has named a price. Until somebody pays it, the toll world is only a forecast. Once one is paid, the idea travels — Zeihan's order is Bab al-Mandab, then the Turkish Straits, then a revival of old Scandinavian dues.

There is one way it reverses. Somebody has to show that ships still pass without paying, which means traffic through Hormuz back at pre-war levels and ordinary insurance rates. Convoys do not count: they are a fee paid to somebody else. Watch the shipping and insurance numbers, not the announcements.

The terms used above

Rate gap
The difference between what two countries pay you for holding their money. If the US pays much more than Japan, money flows out of yen into dollars, and the yen falls. Most currency moves are just this gap widening or narrowing — you rarely need any other explanation.
Strait of Hormuz
The narrow sea passage at the mouth of the Gulf that a large share of the world's traded oil ships through. Its importance is geographic, not political: there is no quick alternative route for most of that volume.
