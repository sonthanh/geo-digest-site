Geo Digest · 2026-W38 · Sep 17–23, 2026

America can no longer protect its allies for free, so it started charging them, and its allies spent the week quietly arranging their own protection.

Its own inspector confirmed that weapons and ammunition are short. Trump says Denmark signed Greenland's security over to Washington, Japan is paying for a chip plant in America, and Canada, Britain and Korea began building without Washington.

First, the rule that never changes

The rule

Q: What does a protector actually sell?

A: Not victory. It sells its presence, paid for every day, whether or not anything happens that day.

Q: What happens when it can no longer pay that daily cost, but is still the strongest one on the street?

A: It stops giving the protection away and starts charging for it. Nothing is announced. The bill arrives as territory, as factories, as tariffs.

Think of it like this. A guard who can no longer walk the whole street stops walking it, and sends the shopkeepers an invoice anyway. They pay for a while, because he is still the biggest man there. Then they start hiring their own.

This week the guard is the United States, and three of the shops signed their own arrangements in five days.

The whole week as one picture

  1. Why is fuel still so expensive where I live?Because the one pipeline carrying Saudi oil around the blocked strait is still out after a drone attack — experts say it could remove as much as 3.6 million barrels a day — and Riyadh then cut supplies to Europe. Physical cargoes went above $120 a barrel.
  2. Who was supposed to stop that happening?The United States. This week its own Pentagon inspector confirmed that it is short of weapons and ammunition and cannot replace them fast enough, and NZZ, the Swiss daily, reported that the big Gulf bases can no longer be defended. The promise is now unable, not unwilling.
  3. If it cannot protect, what does it do instead?It charges. Trump says Denmark has signed over permanent control of Greenland's security. Japan is paying for a chip plant in America as part of its tariff settlement. Neither of those defends anything. Both are payments.
  4. What do the paying countries do about that?They start building without it. In one week Canada moved toward joining the EU as an associate member, Britain asked into a Canada-led defence bank and Europe's industrial scheme, and South Korea publicly refused to send combat troops to the Gulf.
  5. Why does any of this change what I pay to borrow?Because fuel raises the price of everything, and central banks answer that by making money dearer. The Fed raised rates over the president's objection, and Japan went to 1.25%. All three big central banks are now raising at once — the first time in the modern era.
  6. So what reaches Vietnam?Two things. Fuel, because petrol and diesel costs across Southeast Asia have exploded since the war began and protests followed. And money, because Monday's upgrade to emerging-market status brings in investors who arrive for a label and leave much faster than a factory does.
  7. And what would change the answer?One ally refusing to pay a bill in public, and not being punished for it. Until then the selling works. Watch for a refusal with a number attached — a plant not built, a base not granted — never a speech.

The weakest link, and what the picture shows. The arrow from selling protection to the Canada–EU association is the weak one: no source joined them, and the association has been in preparation for months. Watch whether the signed text names defence or only trade. Everything else converges on one node — a protector that cannot protect starts charging, and every country downstream is now paying twice: once for the bill, once for the substitute.

Now, step by step

Vietnam

The mechanism

Q: What does a poor country actually sell to foreign money?

A: Not this year's growth. The promise that its government can do ordinary things, reliably, for years.

Q: What breaks that promise fastest?

A: Not a war or a tariff. A small failure at home at something simple, because anyone can check it.

Think of it like this. A landlord shows investors a beautiful building, and the leases are signed. Then the tenants find the lift broken and the post undelivered. Nobody cancels that day. They simply stop recommending the building.

This week the building is Vietnam. On Monday the index company FTSE Russell moved it up a class, from "frontier" to "secondary emerging market". The broken lift is 3.9 million missing schoolbooks.

  1. On Monday FTSE Russell, whose index lists big funds must follow, moved Vietnam up from "frontier" to "secondary emerging market". That is the best stock-market news Vietnam has had in years.becausethe upgrade puts Vietnam on lists that funds must buy from. That money comes for a label and leaves for a headline. Vietnam Weekly, Sep 17
  2. So the state's ordinary work now matters more, and it failed at one. Two weeks into the school year Vietnam was still about 3.9 million textbooks short, worst in Ho Chi Minh City. The prime minister had to step in.becauselast year's merger of provinces and a single state publisher broke the delivery chain. Books were missing, not money. Vietnam Weekly, Sep 17
  3. So the Party answered with words rather than books. It launched a new campaign of intensified ideological work, with the aim of refuting every "hostile viewpoint".becausea campaign of words is what a state has left when the thing it needed, delivery, is the thing that just failed. Vietnam Weekly, Sep 15
  4. So why answer with words, and why now? The stated aim is to refute "hostile viewpoints". No source reports more. The timing is the complaint underneath: Hanoi named the official in the youth-led "Tree 55" case and said he faces no criminal charge. That settled nothing.becausethe complaint had moved past one fatal crash to whether the rules bind the powerful. Words cannot answer that. Fulcrum, Sep 14
  5. But the state's next test is fuel, not books. Petrol and diesel prices have exploded across Southeast Asia since the Iran war began, with protests in several countries as fishing and farming stopped paying.becauseVietnam buys refined fuel through the same disrupted routes, so a Gulf pipeline sets the cost of a fishing trip in Kiên Giang province. NZZ, Sep 17
  6. So outsiders still bet on Vietnam anyway. Japan made it the first project of a new ¥1.5 trillion Asian energy partnership, supporting crude oil purchases for the Nghi Sơn refinery.becauseevery country wants a second supplier, and Vietnam is the one address both sides can use. That bet rests on the state that missed the books. East Asia Forum, Sep 18
What most people missThe loud Vietnam story is the upgrade, and it reads as arrival. The quiet one is what kind of money it brings. Factory money is slow to come and slow to leave. Index money comes for a label and goes on a headline, and it moves first when rates rise everywhere at once.

Switzerland

The mechanism

Q: What is neutrality actually made of?

A: Two things a country buys for itself: its own defence, and its own stable prices. Neither is a declaration.

Q: What happens if it stops buying one of them?

A: Nothing, for a long time. Then it finds that it depends on another country's army, like everyone else.

Think of it like this. A house on a quiet street keeps its own generator and its own lock. One year the lock fitter is caught overcharging, and the family argues about him for so long that no lock is fitted at all. The street is still quiet. The house is simply not locked.

This week the lock fitter is the American F-35 jet deal, and Switzerland spent the week arguing about him. The generator is the strong franc, and it still runs.

  1. The former head of Switzerland's federal audit office, which checks state spending, warned about F-35 cost overruns in 2022 and was ignored. This week he went public: «Die Bevölkerung sollte blinde Politiker nicht mehr wählen» (the population should stop electing blind politicians).becausethe question is no longer the jet's price but whether the people who signed can be trusted. NZZ, Sep 18
  2. So parliament argues about the investigation, not the aircraft. A Social Democrat senator and a People's Party member agree the fixed price was a fiasco, and split on whether a full inquiry by parliament is needed.becausean inquiry runs for years and freezes decisions while it runs. Buying new weapons stops without anyone voting to stop it. NZZ, Sep 17
  3. So why fight over an inquiry, and why now? Both sides agree the fixed price failed, so the open question is who signed it and why. The timing is step 1: the man who warned in 2022 spoke this week.becausean inquiry answers who knew, not what the jet costs, and it holds the purchase still while it runs. NZZ, Sep 17
  4. But the government refused the other security purchase on offer the same week. The Federal Council, Switzerland's cabinet, rejected the People's Party proposal for systematic border checks with no alternative, calling them «höchst problematisch» (highly problematic) because they put Schengen membership at risk.becauseSchengen is Europe's zone with no checks at inner borders. The open border is worth more than the control. NZZ, Sep 18
  5. So the only protection that still works is one Switzerland already owns: a strong currency. Swiss prices rose 0.4% a year since 2010, against 2.2% in the euro countries, mostly because a strong franc makes imports cheaper.becausewhile other central banks raise rates into a fuel shock, the franc cuts the price of the same fuel before it reaches a Swiss shop. SNB, Sep 13
  6. But a strong franc cannot buy a jet or a border. The proposal for a public vote on neutrality is being campaigned on as a question about Swiss history, not about what Switzerland can defend.becausethe two things that pay for neutrality were both left unbought in one week, while the argument about it stayed about identity. NZZ, Sep 18
What most people missSwitzerland argues loudly about neutrality as an identity and fails quietly to buy any of it. A scandal stalled its own defence purchase, so it sits where every charged ally sits, except that nobody charges it yet.

United States

The mechanism

Q: What is the most expensive thing a great power owns?

A: A promise it must keep every single day, in places where nothing is happening.

Q: What does it do when it cannot keep the promise but still needs the income from it?

A: It narrows what the promise covers and raises the price for the customers who cannot leave.

Think of it like this. An insurance company that owes more than it has in the bank never says so. It quietly shortens the list of what it covers, and sends a bigger bill to the customers who have nowhere else to go.

This week the insurance company is the United States. The shortened list is the Gulf. The bigger bill is Greenland, a chip plant and a tariff list.

  1. The Pentagon's own inspector confirmed that the Iran war has left the United States short of weapons and ammunition, and unable to replace them fast enough. That contradicts the president's claim of "virtually limitless" supplies.becausethis is the protector's own auditor, not an outside estimate. Unwilling changes with an election. Unable changes on a factory schedule measured in years. BBC, Sep 15
  2. So the bases the promise runs through no longer work as promised. Leaked photographs from people serving there showed a radar aircraft cut apart and flattened buildings, and NZZ, the Swiss daily, reported that the big Gulf bases can no longer be defended.because«Der amerikanische Schutzschirm wurde in diesem Krieg zerschmettert» (the American protective umbrella was shattered in this war). NZZ, Sep 16
  3. And no working staff is left in Washington to decide what to do about it. Peter Zeihan, an American analyst, reports only about 1,200 of the top 6,000 senior government posts are filled, with "no layer between them and the White House".becausethat is capacity destroyed, not bad choices. He puts recovery at "a couple of years just to find the people". Peter Zeihan, Sep 14
  4. So the protection is sold instead of given. Trump says Denmark agreed to hand Washington "permanent control over security, and all other needs, in Greenland". Copenhagen has not confirmed the terms.becausecontrol of an ally's territory changed hands by signature, not by force, and that is the cheapest way to take land. BBC, Sep 19
  5. So why charge Denmark, and why now? No source reports Washington's own reason. The sources give the position it charges from: its own inspector confirmed the shortage (step 1), and no staff exists to write a strategy (step 3).becausea state that cannot make weapons or plans must still produce something. This week it produced bills, with signing expected in New York next week. BBC, Sep 19
  6. And allies whose land is not wanted pay in factories instead. Japan and the United States are negotiating a chip plant in America, possibly worth $19 billion, as part of the deal that settled Japan's tariffs.becausea tariff settled with a factory is a payment with an address. It builds capacity inside the protector, not the protected country. Nikkei Asia, Sep 17
What most people missThe week reads as strength: Greenland signed, a tariff weapon armed. Those are invoices, not capabilities, and an invoice works only while the other side has nowhere else to go. The tell nobody priced is domestic: the White House barred CNN, MS Now and Politico in the same week FT, Sep 18.

China

The mechanism

Q: How does a challenger gain in a week it never fought?

A: By selling what the fighting uses up, and buying what the fighting leaves unguarded.

Q: Is that the same as being ready?

A: No. It means its costs are not rising. It says nothing about whether its own army works.

Think of it like this. When a fire burns down a street, the builder's yard sells out. The builder is not braver than anyone. He simply had the materials. But he sacked his foreman last month, and the new one has never run a job.

This week the yard is China, and the new foreman is a military command that Xi is still rebuilding.

  1. New orders at Chinese shipyards nearly tripled as the war on Iran redrew the world's energy routes.becausewhen cargo must take the longer way round, somebody has to build the extra ships, and the yards with spare capacity are Chinese. Nikkei Asia, Sep 18
  2. So the same war that pays China is how Washington now charges it. Trump signed a bill that allows tariffs of up to 100% on the largest buyers of Russian energy, mainly China and India.becausethe Ukraine war became a trade war against countries not fighting in it. China is paid for the ships and billed for the oil in one week. Nikkei Asia, Sep 18
  3. And Washington's other lever on China, the limits on chip sales meant to hold it back, produced a competitor instead. Huawei launched eleven AI chips and said that demand at home already exceeds what it can supply.becausea market large enough and cut off long enough builds its own chips. Nikkei Asia, Sep 17
  4. So why refuse calls to slow AI, and why now? No source reports Beijing's stated reason. The sources show the position it refuses from: the limit on Chinese computing moved from American permission to Chinese capacity, and a Trump–Xi summit is ahead.becausethe refusal came just before that summit, and a country whose demand outruns its supply gains nothing by slowing. Nikkei Asia, Sep 17
  5. But those gains rest on an army being taken apart. Xi's purges, the removal of senior officers, took out the most senior uniformed officer in the force, General Zhang Youxia, along with General Liu Zhenli.becauseForeign Affairs, an American journal, reads the purges as a discipline system working as designed, not as weakness. Either way the command is rebuilt this year. Geopolitical Futures, Sep 15
  6. And China still stores its winnings in the other side's money. Setser and Etra, writing for the Council on Foreign Relations, find China's state banks hold roughly $3.3 trillion of foreign assets, about 70% in dollars, outside the central bank.becauseleaving the dollar means finding somewhere else to put $3.3 trillion, and no such place exists. China keeps lending the proceeds back. CFR, Sep 15
What most people missChina's week looks like a run of free wins, and somebody else's war paid for most of them. The one that is not free: Huawei says home demand for its AI chips already exceeds supply. The limit on Chinese computing moved from American permission to Chinese capacity, which is a slower problem to fix.

What most people miss

The part most people miss

The place a problem appears is almost never where it was caused, and this week the widest gap is in the electricity queue. Hồ Quốc Tuấn reads the AI build differently. Its binding limit moved from money to permits and power. Texas is auditing 474 GW of grid connection requests, and 90% of them are data centres. New York paused approvals above 50 MW Đọc Chậm, Sep 12. When that bites, it will not look like a permit. It will look like a cancelled order in Taiwan, a weak quarter in Korea, a slower electronics month in Vietnam.

The second miss is where everyone is looking in the money. The usual test of the dollar's place is the share of official reserves held in it. That number stayed near 57%. Setser and Etra show the money went instead to state banks, pension funds and sovereign funds, which sell when the return disappoints. The break will therefore show in American asset prices while the reserve table stays flat.

So what would change the answer

The one step: whether allies keep paying the bill instead of leaving. Greenland signed over, a chip plant paid as tribute, tariffs aimed at third countries — all of it works only while the countries being charged have nowhere else to go.

You would see it first in one of these three:

If you see thisThen expect
The Greenland text is published and names what Denmark and Greenland receive in returnIt was a bargain, not a bill, and the selling has limits. If it names only obligations, expect the same shape offered to a second ally within weeks.
A named ally refuses a demanded plant, base or fund share in public, and nothing happens to itThe charging stops working. Washington then has to pay for its own protection at the moment it can least afford to.
A budget line for interceptor and munitions production with money actually appropriatedThe inability becomes temporary. Everything on this page slows down, though not for two or three years.
If it holdsevery ally does what Denmark, Japan, Canada, Britain and Korea each did this week: pay the bill in public and build the alternative in private. Nothing is announced, and the cost lands on whoever is nearest and cannot walk away.
If it reversesonly two things count, and statements are not among them. Either the United States puts real money in its budget to make more weapons and ammunition, so the protection becomes real again, or an ally publicly refuses a demanded payment and is not punished for it.

Now you try

The move to copy this week. When a powerful country demands something new, do not ask what it wants. Ask what it can no longer do, and who is being charged for the gap. Every step on this page came out of that question.

Three questions to ask of any story next week:

1. Is this actor unwilling, or unable? Unwilling reverses with an election; unable reverses on a production schedule. 2. Who signed, and what could they have done instead? A deal a country could have refused is a bargain. One it could not is a bill. 3. What would I actually see if I am right? A number, a date or a shipment. Never a statement.

Now try it on one we have not answered. Vietnam's top leader, Tô Lâm, went to New York for the UN General Assembly this week, seeking a meeting with Trump that has not been confirmed. Run the three questions on it. If that meeting happens, what does Vietnam hand over — and would you recognise the payment if it were dressed as an investment announcement?

Write your answer down before next Saturday. Next week's report will tell you whether the meeting happened and what was announced, and that is the only way to find out whether your reasoning was right or merely comfortable.
