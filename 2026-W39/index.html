Geo Digest · 2026-W39 · Sep 21–27, 2026

The Iran war now costs both sides more than it gains, so this week both started looking for a way out.

The bill arrived at the pump, in the price of money and at the summit table, where Washington needed Beijing more than Beijing needed Washington.

First, the rule that never changes

The rule

Q: When does a war start to end?

A: Not when one side wins. When staying in costs more than getting out, for both sides at the same time.

Think of it like this. Two neighbours go to court over a fence. After a year, the lawyers' bills cost more than the fence, and both of them start to look for a settlement. This week the fence is the .

The whole week as one picture

  1. Why is diesel so expensive this week?Because two wars remove it from the same market. The Iran war closed the Strait of Hormuz, and Ukraine's drones keep hitting Russian refineries. In America, diesel is up 79% since the Iran strikes in February. In Scotland, pump diesel passed £2 a litre.
  2. Who pays for that besides drivers?Governments. When fuel pushes prices up, lenders ask more interest to protect their money. America now pays more to borrow for ten years than at any time since 2007. It also spent more than $33bn on one military operation against Iran.
  3. So does anyone want the war to stop?Both sides now do. Iran offered to reopen the strait within seven days in return for American concessions, and the two held their first talks since June. Trump also called off strikes on the Houthis, Iran's allies in Yemen, after Saudi Arabia's crown prince asked him to.
  4. Why did China matter so much at the summit, then?Because China is the bank Iran uses to reach the world. George Friedman argues that China can squeeze Iran by closing that door, and that it will ask for Taiwan in return. The summit settled neither. It gave small tariff cuts and bought time.
  5. So what reaches Vietnam and Switzerland?Two different bills. Vietnam gets dearer fuel and dearer money just as it pays for a huge building plan with land instead of cash. Switzerland gets almost nothing yet: its central bank kept rates at zero, because Swiss prices barely moved.
  6. What can change the answer?Ships moving through Hormuz again. A statement from Tehran or Washington does not count. Watch loaded tankers leaving the Gulf, and the diesel price in the weeks after. The exit is real only when both move. Until then, the bill keeps growing.

The weakest link, and what the picture shows. The arrow from higher borrowing costs to Vietnam's land-paid building plan is the weak one: no source joined them, and Ho Chi Minh City started the land deals in April, before this week's jump in rates. Watch whether a flagship Vietnamese project switches from private to state or bank money. The rest converges on one node: the war's cost now reaches Washington's own borrowing, and that is what sent it to Beijing for help.

Now, step by step

Vietnam

The mechanism

Q: How does a middle-sized country stay safe between two giants?

A: By being useful to both and depending on neither.

Q: What does that cost?

A: Money at home. Every new customer abroad needs roads, ports and power at home, and somebody has to pay for them.

Think of it like this. A small shop sits between two big supermarkets. It survives by selling what both of them need. To look good for both, it rebuilds the shop, and pays the builder with part of its own backyard.

This week the shop is Vietnam. The backyard is land in Ho Chi Minh City.

  1. Vietnam's top leader, Tô Lâm, met the US trade representative Jamieson Greer in New York. Vietnamese media reported that the US-Vietnam trade deal is close to completion.becausea finished deal fixes the tariff, the import tax, that Vietnamese goods pay to enter America. That tax is the base of the factory model. The Diplomat, Sep 22
  2. But Vietnam's deal depends on a bigger one. Trump and Xi extended their own trade truce only for a short time, and left technology and Taiwan for later.becauseFulcrum says the truce expires on 10 November. If it lapses, heavy tariffs and limits on minerals and technology can return, and Vietnam sits in the middle. Fulcrum, Sep 21
  3. So Hanoi adds partners that belong to neither giant. Vietnam and Canada raised their ties to a "strategic partnership", the second-highest level in Vietnam's ranking of foreign relationships.becausea third customer reduces the damage from a hostile giant. The Diplomat describes the deal as two middle powers spreading their bets in a disordered world. The Diplomat, Sep 25
  4. So Vietnam must build fast, and it pays with land. Ho Chi Minh City started four projects worth VND142 trillion (about $5.5bn) and paid the builders with 33 plots of land instead of money.becauseevery new customer abroad needs roads, ports and power at home. Fulcrum says this revives a build-then-pay-in-land model that the 2020 law on public-private deals had excluded. Fulcrum, Sep 23
  5. So why pay in land, and why now? Fulcrum says the land pays for Vietnam's VND8.22 quadrillion public investment plan for 2026-30, and that plan started this year. The city began the land deals in April.becauseland pays the builder without cash from the budget now, but it brings debt and corruption risks. Whether the city lacks cash is not reported. Fulcrum, Sep 23
  6. And the state runs this the same way it ran the schools. It merged more than 11,000 schools in about 14 months before the school year began on 5 September.becauseFulcrum reads both as Tô Lâm's governing style: merge institutions, set number targets and tight deadlines, then catch problems afterwards. That works until a target meets a falling land price. Fulcrum, Sep 22
What most people missMost people read the Iran war as only a fuel bill for Vietnam. It is also changing Vietnam's power plan. Since 28 February, six Southeast Asian economies issued 37 renewable and electrification plans and only 29 fossil-fuel plans. Vietnam is one of the six Nikkei Asia, Sep 25. A country that imports fuel through a closed strait now treats sun and wind as a security question.

Switzerland

The mechanism

Q: The neighbourhood gets less safe. What protects a small, rich country?

A: Two things it can pay for itself: a currency people trust, and a defence it controls.

Q: And when one of the two depends on a foreigner?

A: Then an argument about neutrality is really an argument about who you depend on.

Think of it like this. A family has good savings in the bank. Its new door lock comes from a supplier abroad, and after signing, the supplier and the family argue about the price. The street outside gets rougher. The savings still work. The lock is not fitted yet.

This week the savings are the Swiss franc. The lock is the American F-35 fighter jet.

  1. The US ambassador to NATO, the Western military alliance, told European allies that Europe is now Washington's fourth priority.becauseSwitzerland is not in NATO, but its defence plan assumes a protected Europe around it. That protected Europe now has less American attention behind it. NZZ, Sep 25
  2. So Switzerland's own defence purchase matters more, and it is stuck. Swiss officials visited the F-35 factory during the dispute over the jet's "fixed price".becausea fixed price means the seller pays any cost overrun. The dispute is whether that promise holds, so the final cost of the jets is open. NZZ, Sep 22
  3. But the second protection, the currency, still does its job. On 24 September the Swiss National Bank kept its interest rate at 0% and said it stays ready to act in currency markets.becauseoil lifted Swiss inflation only from 0.6% in the spring to 0.8% in August, while the European and American central banks both raised rates. SNB, Sep 24
  4. So the economy keeps earning while Europe worries. In the second quarter Switzerland earned CHF 24bn more from the rest of the world than it spent there, CHF 13bn more than a year before.becausegoods trade drove the rise, including trading in raw materials by Swiss firms. The bank also says world factories gain from demand for technology, defence and infrastructure. SNB, Sep 22
  5. But the money does not answer the defence question, and voters get that question on 27 September. They decide on Christoph Blocher's , a proposal to change the rules on Swiss neutrality, one day after this week closes.becauseNZZ reported the vote in August, so the date comes from the vote calendar, not from this week's news. NZZ, Aug 26
  6. So why put neutrality to a vote at all, and why now? This week's sources do not report what Blocher's supporters want the rule to change. The timing is what this week adds: Swiss voters answer that question in the week Washington put Europe fourth.becausean argument about neutrality is an argument about who Switzerland depends on, and this week the American protection looks less certain. NZZ, Sep 25
What most people missIn a war year you expect frightened money to rush into the franc and push it up. This week the Swiss National Bank said the franc was weaker. It welcomed that, because a weaker franc helps the economy SNB, Sep 24. The traditional safe place was not used as one this week. Foreign money went into American shares instead.

United States

The mechanism

Q: How does a rich country pay for a long war?

A: Mostly by borrowing.

Q: And when the lenders get nervous?

A: They ask for more interest. Then the war costs twice: once at the front, and once in the budget.

Think of it like this. A family pays for a long court case with a credit card. The bank raises the card's interest rate. Now the family wants a settlement. It is not losing the case. The card now costs too much.

This week the family is the United States. The card is the money it borrows by selling government bonds.

  1. America spent more than $33bn on Operation Epic Fury, its military operation against Iran, and even more on defending against Iran's attacks.becausea war fought far away costs money every day, in fuel, weapons and ships. NZZ, Sep 22
  2. So lenders asked for more. The interest rate on ten-year US government bonds, the loans Washington sells to borrow, hit 5.22%, the first time since June 2007.becauseNikkei reports that investors fear oil keeps inflation high and keeps central bank rates high. So they demand more interest to lend for ten years. Nikkei Asia, Sep 25
  3. And foreign money chose shares over loans. Foreigners bought a record $940bn of US stocks in the year to July, while they bought less US government debt.becausewhen foreign buyers buy less government debt, the government must pay more to find other lenders. FT, Sep 26
  4. So Washington cannot fight everywhere. Trump cancelled planned strikes on the Houthis, Iran's allies in Yemen, after a personal plea from Saudi Arabia's crown prince, Mohammed bin Salman.becausePhillips O'Brien says Trump was right to cancel the strikes, but did it in the worst possible way. Phillips P. O'Brien, Sep 23
  5. So why cancel the strikes, and why now? O'Brien says the US is short of ammunition, and Republican support for the Iran war fell from 73% to 60%.becausea country short of ammunition and losing its own voters cannot fight the Houthis and Iran at once. The limit is capacity, not choice. Phillips P. O'Brien, Sep 23
  6. So it looks for a way out. American and Iranian officials held their first talks since the ceasefire collapsed in June.becausewhen both the fighting and the borrowing get dearer, talking becomes cheaper than the next strike. BBC, Sep 23
What most people missAmerica's money strain appeared in Tokyo, not Washington. Trump told Japan's prime minister Takaichi that "the weak yen is making trade difficult for the U.S." Then the two finance ministers agreed that an undervalued yen "is a problem" Nikkei Asia, Sep 25. A stronger yen needs Japan to act, and Japan is the largest foreign lender to the US government.

China

The mechanism

Q: Why does the stronger side in a deal still hurry?

A: Because it has its own weak spots at home.

Q: How can you tell?

A: Look at what it does at home in the same week. Ignore what it says at the table.

Think of it like this. A shopkeeper knows his rival is in debt, so he bargains hard. But his own storeroom is overfull. And he fired his two managers the week before the meeting.

This week the shopkeeper is China.

  1. Xi made his first state visit to Washington in about ten years. The deal cut tariffs on $30bn of ordinary goods each way, added Chinese coal purchases, and left Taiwan and technology for later.becausethe White House fact sheet lists only low-risk goods: US farm goods, and Chinese toys and small appliances. The hard questions stayed open. Nikkei Asia, Sep 26
  2. So why did Xi go to Washington, and why now? George Friedman says China also needs the Iran war to end, because its biggest customer is the United States, and that customer now pays for a war.becauseFriedman argues the summit was really about the Gulf. Both sides share an interest in ending the war, and the war is now. Geopolitical Futures, Sep 24
  3. And China holds the stronger hand at that table. Alicia Garcia-Herrero argues that high US borrowing costs and Gulf-driven inflation leave Trump no room to fight China on trade.becausea country that pays more to borrow and fights a war cannot also afford a trade war. Nikkei Asia, Sep 25
  4. And China has something Washington wants. Friedman argues that China can squeeze Iran by closing Chinese banks to it. The likely price is a pause in US arms sales to Taiwan.becauseIran reaches the world's money mostly through Chinese banks, so China decides whether Iran gets paid. Geopolitical Futures, Sep 24
  5. But the strong hand has weak spots at home. Xi fired the two chiefs of staff of his army a few days before the summit.becauseFriedman reads the timing as insecurity, not strength: "He's not firing generals because he's feeling secure." The number of officers fired is Friedman's own claim. Geopolitical Futures, Sep 24
  6. And the second weak spot is in the factories: China makes more cars than its people buy. Beijing hurried the merger of the Toyota partnerships of two state carmakers, GAC and FAW.becauseNikkei reads it as a sign that local governments can no longer carry the losses from too many car factories. More mergers are coming. Nikkei Asia, Sep 25
What most people missAt the White House Xi called for keeping AI "under human control" Nikkei Asia, Sep 25. That sounds like caution. Peter Zeihan puts China's chips at the older 28-nanometre level, about 36 months behind. He says China will reject any pact to slow AI Zeihan, Sep 24. Asking for rules on the race while refusing to slow down is what the side behind in a race does.

Seen from their own capital

  • Tehran — it wants the blockade lifted before shortages break the home front. Medicines already run short under US sanctions FT, Sep 26.
  • Riyadh — it wants the Houthis pushed back but cannot do it alone, and its partners are holding back NZZ, Sep 24.
  • Tokyo — it wants a bigger budget without new deficit bonds. So it plans a separate "bridging bond" account Nikkei Asia, Sep 24.
  • Warsaw — it wants American bases on its soil. Friedman calls Poland the new West Germany, the country any Russian attack must beat first Geopolitical Futures, Sep 21.

What most people miss

The part most people miss

The Iran war now shows up in house prices, far from the Gulf. Nikkei reports that the bond sell-off already hits housing markets. Mortgage rates follow government borrowing costs Nikkei Asia, Sep 25. A family that never buys diesel still pays for the strait through its mortgage.

The second miss: lenders no longer rank countries the old way. Lowy finds that Western borrowing costs rise fast. Some African governments now borrow more cheaply than before Lowy Institute, Sep 22. Investors now price a government's politics and promises, not its old place in the queue.

So what would change the answer

The one step: whether the war's cost now outruns its purpose for Washington and Tehran at the same time. One side tiring is not enough. Iran's offer and America's called-off strikes say both are tiring, but NZZ also reports that Trump reportedly rejected the ceasefire offer.

You will see it first in one of these three:

If it reversesonly actions count: new US strikes, a wider Houthi campaign against Saudi Arabia, or the strait staying shut while the talks continue. Then the bill keeps rising, and Beijing's hand gets stronger.

Now you try

The move to copy this week. Do not ask who is winning a long war. Ask who is paying, and whether both bills rise together.

Three questions to ask of any story next week:

1. Who pays for this, and in what form: money, fuel, soldiers or votes? 2. Is that cost rising for one side only, or for both? Only a cost that rises for both ends a war. 3. What will I actually see if I am right? A number, a date or a shipment. Never a statement.

Now try it on one we have not answered. Swiss voters decide on the neutrality initiative on Sunday 27 September. That is one day after this week closes. Run the three questions on it. Who pays for a yes, and who pays for a no? Then say what you expect: does it pass, and does it win a majority of cantons as well as of voters?

Write your answer down before next Saturday. Next week's report will give the result, and that is the only way to find out whether your reasoning was right or only comfortable.

The terms used above

Strait of Hormuz
The narrow sea passage at the mouth of the Gulf that a large share of the world's traded oil ships through. Its importance is geographic, not political: there is no quick alternative route for most of that volume.
Neutrality initiative
A Swiss popular vote proposal to write strict neutrality into the constitution — which would bind future governments rather than leaving neutrality a matter of policy. Launched from the political right, so left-wing groups backing it is a genuine change in its odds.
