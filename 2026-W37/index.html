Everyone can now close a sea route and nobody can open one, so protection stopped being a promise and became a timetable.

Saudi Arabia shut its own pipeline around the Strait. Washington cut tanker air cover to two windows a day. The Houthis took the Red Sea coastline.

First, the rule that never changes

The rule

Q: What keeps a trade route open?

A: Not law and not distance. Somebody strong enough to be there, paying the running cost every day, whether or not anything happens.

Q: What happens when several powers can close a route and none can afford to hold it open?

A: Safety stops being a guarantee and becomes a schedule. You are covered at certain hours and you carry the risk the rest of the time.

Think of it like this. A night bus runs through a rough district, and the police used to patrol it all night. Now they patrol from eight to nine and from two to three. Nothing was cancelled and nobody was told. But every driver reprices the fare, and some stop driving.

This week the route is the , the narrow sea passage Gulf oil leaves through. The two patrol hours are literal.

Now, step by step

Vietnam

The mechanism

Q: What does a small country win when two giants fight over trade?

A: The factories that leave the loser. That prize is real.

Q: What does the prize cost?

A: The small country becomes the one name that grew on the winner's own list of problems.

Think of it like this. Two neighbours quarrel, and the customers of one walk down the road to a small shop. The shop is happy. Then the angry neighbour opens his own books and finds one name that grew. He sends an inspector to ask where the goods on those shelves were made.

This week the small shop is Vietnam. It sold the United States $114bn more than it bought, the largest gap of any country. The inspector is a ruling on where a good really comes from.

  1. Vietnam sold the United States $114bn more goods than it bought from it in the first half of 2026. That is the largest gap of any country, ahead of Mexico at $103bn and China at $74bn.becauseVietnam pays an effective tax of about 6% on those goods, and China pays 23%. So the cheapest factory move on earth still ends in Vietnam. Lowy Institute, Sep 11
  2. So the prize became the danger. The biggest number at the lowest tax rate goes first in line for a ruling on where its goods really come from.becausea tax rate is a cost that Hanoi can argue down. A ruling on origin says the goods were never Vietnamese, and no tax schedule can offset that. Trigger C10 watches for this. Lowy Institute, Sep 11
  3. And that ruling will hit a boom that only outsiders feel. First-half growth of 8.2% came almost entirely from foreign-owned and state-favoured firms, while small Vietnamese firms lost credit and land.becausea boom carried by outsiders leaves when the rule on origin changes. East Asia Forum, Sep 11
  4. And shared or not, the boom runs on imported fuel. Brent crude, the world oil price, crossed $100 and then reached $105 as the passage out of the Gulf effectively closed.becauseVietnam buys both crude oil and refined fuel through that passage, and nobody can build a refinery inside a crisis. Trigger C9 sits here. BBC, Sep 10
  5. So Hanoi adds suppliers that nobody can take away. Vietnam's top leader, Tô Lâm, went to Moscow to widen nuclear and oil-and-gas work with Putin, then flew straight on to France.becausetwo capitals on one trip is the whole doctrine. Vietnam does not choose a side. It refuses to have only one. Nikkei Asia, Sep 10
  6. So why go to Moscow, and why now? The report names energy as the purpose and gives no stated reason from Hanoi. The timing is on this page: oil is at $105, the passage is closed, and nobody can build a refinery inside a crisis.becausea new supplier is the one insurance that a fuel buyer can still arrange while the passage is closed. Nikkei Asia, Sep 10
What most people missThe loud Vietnam story is the record surplus, and it reads as a win. The quiet one is that the rate producing it is set by how close Washington thinks Hanoi stands, not by what Vietnam sells — and an origin ruling never changes the published rate, so it will move without a headline. Two more items belong in the file. Vietnam's $4bn Cà Mau–Hòn Khoai bridge and dual-use port corridor pushes its contest with China off the water and into Cambodian ground. Switzerland

The mechanism

Q: Why does a neutral country still need its own weapons?

A: Because neutrality is not a label that a country announces. It is a defence that the country pays for, and the payment must arrive.

Q: What breaks that faster, a war or a bad invoice?

A: A bad invoice. A country can argue about a war for years. A price that was never fixed destroys the trust that the paying depends on.

Think of it like this. A village votes a tax to build its own fire station. Then the village learns that the builder's fixed price was never fixed, and that one man in the office knew. The fire risk did not change that day. The willingness to pay did, and the station is not built.

This week the builder is the American F-35 fighter programme. The tax is a Swiss rise in value-added tax, the sales tax on most goods.

  1. The NZZ investigation found that the defence ministry and the army's purchasing agency held to the claim of a fixed price for the F-35 fighter jet against nearly all the evidence. One purchasing official held the details.becausethe NZZ commentary says the affair destroyed trust at the most delicate moment of army financing, while the threat keeps rising. NZZ, Sep 12
  2. So the money followed the trust out. The security committee of the upper house of the Swiss parliament moved to separate the special army fund from the value-added tax rise that was meant to pay for it.becausethe army build-up lost its source of money without a single vote on the build-up itself. NZZ, Sep 10
  3. So why unhook the fund, and why now? No report here gives the committee's stated reason. The timing is reported: the F-35 price claim collapsed in the same week, at what the NZZ calls the most delicate moment of army financing.becauseonce the buyer's own numbers are in doubt, no chamber will tie an unpopular tax to the purchase. NZZ, Sep 12
  4. And neutrality itself lost its main selling point in the same week. A new analysis found that the claim that neutrality lets Switzerland do business with everybody is false.becausethat claim is the business argument at the centre of the , the public vote now in campaign. Without it, neutrality is a cost and not a discount. NZZ, Sep 12
  5. So the voters can see the gap. Only about a third of Swiss voters believe that their politics can react quickly to a global crisis.becausethey have just watched a fighter price, an army fund and a neutrality argument come apart in one week, with no decision taken on any of them. NZZ, Sep 10
  6. So the central bank is the only Swiss institution left that acts. The chairman of the Swiss National Bank told business leaders that fuel prices are the main reason Swiss inflation rose, and closed on the slide «Märkte erwarten globalen Anstieg der Zinsen» (markets expect a global rise in interest rates).becausethe bank can act this week without asking anyone. SNB, Sep 11
What most people missSwitzerland debates neutrality as an identity and then has it priced by a foreign invoice and a foreign interest rate. Nobody voted for either. The deeper miss is the sequencing: the F-35 affair is not a scandal that delays rearmament, it is the reason the tax that funds rearmament has just been unhooked — the failure moved from the aircraft to the money in four days. Trigger B23 was written for exactly this shape, and this is its second consecutive week of evidence.

United States

The mechanism

Q: What does a security promise cost?

A: The daily cost of being there, whether or not anything happens that day.

Q: What happens when the daily cost is higher than what you can build?

A: The promise gets rationed. Nobody cancels it and nobody withdraws it. It arrives in smaller amounts, by the hour.

Think of it like this. A landlord promises that the building always has a night watchman. Then the landlord cuts the watchman to two patrols a night. Nobody announced a withdrawal, and the promise is still on paper. But every tenant fits their own lock, and after that the promise is over anyway.

This week the landlord is the United States. The two patrols are literal: protection from air attack for tankers in the Strait of Hormuz, cut to two windows a day.

  1. Washington cut its protection from air attack for commercial tankers in the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow passage that Gulf oil leaves through, to two set windows a day.becausethe FT reports that the cut came after an increase in Iranian attacks. Cover that used to run all day now comes by the hour, and nobody called it a withdrawal. FT, Sep 12
  2. So why ration the cover, and why now? No stated reason is reported. This page reads it as the missile stock: O'Brien counts sixty-plus defensive missiles fired over Jordan on 8 September against twenty incoming, perhaps 5–10% of 700–800 left.becauseIran strikes American partner states so that Washington spends its stock defending them. The attacker pays a fraction of each defending shot. Phillips P. O'Brien, Sep 9
  3. So the partners stopped waiting to be defended. Saudi Arabia shut its own 1,200km , the line that carries 4–5% of world oil around the Strait, after drones launched from Iraq hit it, and did not strike back.becausethe one physical way around a closed passage was closed by its owner. Holding it open cost more than losing it. BBC, Sep 12
  4. And Washington declined to step in itself. Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman pressed Trump directly for American strikes and was offered intelligence and targeting support only.becausea day later the Houthis of Yemen took Perim Island at the mouth of the Bab al-Mandab, the passage into the Red Sea, after taking the port of Mokha. A second sea route changed hands because nobody stepped in. BBC, Sep 11
  5. So the war comes home as a household price. American consumer prices rose 3.4% in the year to August as fuel costs squeezed budgets, and Trump said the war and high oil prices will last until after the November elections.becausehe treats the daily cost as a fixed term that he cannot shorten, and that is an admission. BBC, Sep 11
  6. So borrowing costs rise where the spending cannot stop. The rate the US government pays to borrow for ten years keeps climbing toward 5% despite the finance minister's efforts, and American and British long-term borrowing costs are at their highest in decades.becausean oil shock is landing on a government whose debts leave no room, and its lenders are the one opponent that nobody can talk down. NZZ, Sep 11
What most people missThe week reads as escalation — strikes on five Iranian tankers, hard talk at the 9/11 commemoration. Both are cheap. The expensive things all moved the other way: the cover schedule, the unanswered Saudi request, the interceptor count. The tell nobody priced is domestic. A postal service is building a portal to check mail-in ballot envelopes at the November elections NZZ, Sep 7, and the president has begun promising every adult $5,000 conditional on his party winning BBC, Sep 11. A country rationing protection abroad is bidding for loyalty at home.

China

The mechanism

Q: How do you gain from a fight you never join?

A: You let the other side burn its stock. Then you buy the things it can no longer protect.

Q: Is that the same as being strong?

A: No. It is being the one whose costs are not rising. That is a different thing, and it ends the moment the other side stops spending.

Think of it like this. Two shops on one street run a price war, and both stop repairing their roofs. A third shop across the road sells nothing cheaper and nothing better. It fixes its roof and waits for the rain.

This week the third shop is China. The roof is the oil it buys while others fight, and the memory chips it now makes at a profit.

  1. China went back into the oil market and bought crude as the fighting in the Strait pushed prices up, though its volumes stay below the level before the war.becauseit spent the early war using up its stored oil. Nikkei Asia, Sep 11
  2. So why buy now, into a price spike? Nikkei gives no stated reason from Beijing. It reports the timing: the stored oil is run down and the fighting has not stopped.becausea buyer who expects a spike to end waits it out. Buying into it is what a country does when it expects the spike to last. Nikkei Asia, Sep 11
  3. So China also gains the industries that the West struggles to hold. The Chinese memory-chip maker CXMT now keeps a larger share of each sale as profit than SK Hynix of Korea or Micron of America.becausetaking the profit lead in a business that Korea, America and Japan owned for forty years is no longer a story about government money. Nikkei Asia, Sep 10
  4. So Washington's answer to those gains bought the wrong thing. Zeihan reads the roughly $2bn stockpile of minerals that America built as insurance against China: rare earths and gallium come out as side products of other mining, and "70% on average of critical materials processing is done in China".becausethat makes the stockpile Chinese-processed mine waste, bought as insurance against China. Peter Zeihan, Sep 10
  5. And the one real fix got harder in the same days. Washington banned imports of Canadian spirits, some dairy goods and motorbikes from 29 September, with no trade talks scheduled since negotiations collapsed in late August.becauseZeihan names repaired relations with Canada, Australia and Brazil as the real fix: "maybe stop calling it the 51st state". The mineral plan and the trade policy point opposite ways. BBC, Sep 9
  6. But Beijing's grip on its own gains is looser than the headlines say. Its limits on selling rare earths abroad run through provincial governments, regulators and firms that it does not fully command.becausethat is why the score moves by a tenth and not by a point. The gains are real. The control over them is not fully Beijing's. East Asia Forum, Sep 10
What most people missChina's week looks like a run of free wins, and most of them were paid for by somebody else's spending rather than earned. The item to keep is the one that is not free: domestic capital bid a Chinese AI-chip designer up 179% on its first day in Shanghai. Nikkei Asia, Sep 11 That is the same one-legged market Hồ Quốc Tuấn describes in the West, built inside a closed savings pool — so when the AI group disappoints, China does not escape it either.

Seen from their own capital

  • Tokyo — it optimises for not importing an inflation it cannot control, and the constraint is that just over 70% of its crude comes through the Strait of Hormuz. Producer prices ran +7.6% in August with import prices in yen up 24.8%, so board member MASU warned the closure can force "a rapid policy interest rate hike", and the Bank of Japan is expected to move to 1.25% on 17–18 September. Money growth stayed modest, which tells you this is a cost shock and not a money shock. What Tokyo cannot do is defend the currency and the export economy at once: the post-intervention rise in the yen has already run past the rate most large manufacturers budgeted for.
  • Berlin — it optimises for holding the post-1945 settlement together and has just lost the tool it used. Friedman's reading is that the ban on a distinct German national interest expired with the generation that imposed it, and the AfD's Saxony-Anhalt win is the receipt. The party is repositioning fast: a Bundestag member travelled to Kyiv and says the Moscow-friendly wing is falling silent. The contagion is already abroad, with the Italian right outflanked by a former general polling sharply upward. Berlin cannot lead Europe's rearmament and lose its own east in the same electoral cycle.
  • liveRiyadh — it optimises for keeping its energy system intact and this week discovered the guarantee behind it is advisory. Houthi strikes hit Saudi energy sites, the crown prince asked Washington directly for strikes and received intelligence and targeting help, and then Riyadh shut its own pipeline around the Strait and declined to retaliate. That last decision is the one to read: a state that still believed in the guarantee escalates and lets the patron cover it. What Riyadh cannot do is defend a 1,200km line against drones launched from a neighbouring country, so it took the line out of service instead — and the new Mecca pact, whose credibility gap is a live argument, is what it has left.
  • Ottawa — it optimises for finding a customer base that is not one angry neighbour, and the constraint is arithmetic. Carney convened a summit to raise $1tn of foreign capital by pitching Canada as the safe place away from its own largest trading partner, in the same week Washington banned Canadian spirits, some dairy and motorbikes from 29 September and nine days after Canada was named an essential minerals partner. A Japanese brewer is already moving production out of Canada into the United States. What Ottawa cannot do is out-wait a policy that gets a domestic political return from the damage.

What most people miss

The part most people miss

The place a problem appears is almost never where it was caused, and this week the gap is widest in the dullest document. The Bank of Japan's August producer-price release is not a Japanese story. Import prices in yen up 24.8% is the Strait of Hormuz arriving inside the cost base of the world's largest creditor nation, and the rate rise that follows is the last cheap pool of money in the world being priced up. Every borrower on earth, including Vietnam, pays for that closure through Tokyo without ever reading a Japanese headline.

The second miss is that the protection story is not about willingness. It is tempting to read the two-window tanker schedule and the refusal of the Saudi request as American reluctance, which an election can reverse. O'Brien's count says otherwise: sixty-plus interceptors in a night out of seven or eight hundred. Unwilling reverses with a vote. Unable reverses on a factory schedule measured in years, and that is the version the week supports. Trigger B17 was written for this distinction and remains the highest-value thing on the watch list.

Third, hold on to one asymmetry. Closing something is cheap and opening it is ruinous. The drones that shut a 1,200km pipeline cost a rounding error against the pipeline. The missiles Iran fired at Jordan cost a fraction of what was fired to stop them. The Houthis took a coastline with infantry. Against that sits a defence industrial base that cannot raise output inside an election cycle and a government whose borrowing rate is walking toward 5%. This is not a contest of resolve. It is a contest of unit costs, and the side making the promises is on the wrong side of it.

So what would change the answer

Everything on this page rests on one step: whether protection stays rationed. Two windows a day for tankers, intelligence instead of strikes for Riyadh, and a defensive-missile stock being spent at sixty a night are the same decision taken three times. While that holds, every other actor is rational to shut its own valves, buy its own suppliers and price its own risk — which is precisely what Saudi Arabia, Canada, Japan, Switzerland and Vietnam each did this week.

There are only two ways it reverses. Either American interceptor production gets a dedicated appropriation large enough to change the arithmetic, or somebody pushes the Houthis off the Bab al-Mandab coastline and reopens the Red Sea approach at ordinary insurance rates. Statements do not count. An appropriation and a reopened route do.

The terms used above

Petroline
The Saudi pipeline that carries oil across the country to the Red Sea, so tankers can avoid the Strait of Hormuz entirely. It is the main backup route if the Gulf is blocked — which is exactly why threatening it removes the world's spare option.
Strait of Hormuz
The narrow sea passage at the mouth of the Gulf that a large share of the world's traded oil ships through. Its importance is geographic, not political: there is no quick alternative route for most of that volume.
Neutrality initiative
A Swiss popular vote proposal to write strict neutrality into the constitution — which would bind future governments rather than leaving neutrality a matter of policy. Launched from the political right, so left-wing groups backing it is a genuine change in its odds.
