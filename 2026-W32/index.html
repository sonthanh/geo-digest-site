The American guarantee did not break this week — it ran out.

Six months of war burned a reported ~80% of the advanced interceptor stock and "virtually all" the long-range precision missiles, and everything else on this page (Riyadh calling off a US strike by telephone, Tehran settling the Strait with Oman, Saudi Arabia signing a mutual-defence pact with Turkey and Pakistan, Washington charging Tokyo policy concessions for dollars) is what happens when the people standing under an umbrella discover it is empty rather than merely folded. Seventh live run. Window Aug 1–8; 203 in-window items across 24 sources. W31's through-line was the backstops decline the claim — guarantors that could have covered a shock chose not to. W32's is one step worse and one step more permanent: the backstop cannot cover it. Unwilling is a decision and reverses with an election. Unable is an inventory, and it reverses on a production schedule measured in years.

First, the rule that never changes

The rule

Q: What is a promise of protection really made of?

A: Stock. What you can still fire, divided by how fast the factory replaces it.

Q: Why can a country not simply renew the promise?

A: A promise takes an afternoon. A stockpile takes years.

Q: So how do you know it has expired?

A: Nobody announces it — announcing it is what triggers the repricing. You watch the people who were relying on it. They stop asking, and start buying their own.

Think of it like this. A pharmacy promises to keep everyone's medicine in stock. Quietly, the shelf empties faster than the supplier refills it. Nobody puts up a sign. You find out when the regulars start ordering their own supply from elsewhere.

A promise can be renewed in an afternoon. A stockpile cannot. So the moment the magazine empties faster than the line replaces it, the guarantee expires whether or not anyone announces the expiry — and, crucially, the guarantor has every reason not to announce it, because the announcement is what triggers the repricing. That is why the reliable signal is never the statement. It is the behaviour of the people who were relying on it: they stop asking, and they start buying their own.

Everything below is that one sentence applied to missiles, to dollars, and to rare earths.

Vietnam

The mechanism

Q: What does a country that assembles things actually control?

A: Very little. It buys parts at the world's price and sells the finished goods at its customer's price. It keeps only the gap between them.

Q: Why is that dangerous?

A: Because every shock from outside lands on that thin gap, and none of the levers that would fix it are in Hanoi.

Think of it like this. A tailor buys cloth at whatever the mill charges and sells suits at whatever the shop will pay. When cloth gets dearer, he cannot raise the suit price and he cannot make the cloth. He simply earns less.

What most people missThe trade deficit is being read as an export problem. That is the official answer to who absorbs a geopolitical shock, and the answer is the person with the thinnest balance sheet in the chain.

Switzerland

The mechanism

Q: What does staying neutral actually cost?

A: Real money and real equipment. It is a capability, not a statement.

Q: Why is it so slow to buy in Switzerland?

A: Because the bill does not arrive as a foreign-policy choice. It arrives as a domestic procedure, and procedures have their own speed limit.

What most people missSwitzerland's exposure this week is procedural, not military. If the guarantee everyone else is re-pricing turns out to be genuinely unavailable, the country whose decision cycle is measured in decades will be the last to have re-priced.

China

The mechanism

Q: How do you get power without a navy?

A: Make something enough people need. Then you do not have to reach them at all.

Q: Why is that the cheaper route?

A: Because building a factory that the world depends on costs far less than building a fleet that can sail to it.

Think of it like this. The only supplier of a part every workshop on the street needs never has to leave his own yard. Everybody comes to him.

What most people missThe R&D crossing is being read as a scoreboard. The giveaway is the bigger move. A country that leads on research spend and then prices the output at zero is not trying to win a market — it is trying to remove the price. And the first casualty is not an American lab. It is every mid-income economy whose development plan was to climb from assembly onto software and services, because that rung now has no margin on it. Vietnam and India are more exposed to a free Chinese model than Silicon Valley is.

What most people miss

The part most people miss

The place a problem appears is almost never the place it was caused — and this week the causal place is a factory schedule from ten years ago. The interceptor shortage shows up as a Gulf story in August 2026. It was set by production decisions made between roughly 2015 and 2024, when nobody was buying six months of high-intensity air defence. Nothing Washington decides in the next month changes it.

Run the same move across the page. Vietnam's record trade deficit appears in Hanoi's customs data; it was caused by a narrow sea passage near Oman and an exchange rate in Beijing. The yen's failed defence appears in Tokyo; it was caused by an American war and an American labour market. Thailand's hundred factory closures a month appear in Bangkok; they were caused by a Chinese employment problem. Switzerland's energy vulnerability appears as a parliamentary row over CHF 2.3bn; it was caused by a river and a strait.

And the deepest one: America is not withdrawing from the Middle East. It is running out of the Middle East. From outside the two look identical — fewer strikes, fewer forces, more delegation to partners. They behave completely differently. A withdrawal is a policy and can be reversed by the next president in a week. An inventory shortfall is physics and reverses on a production line's schedule. Everyone reacting this week — Riyadh, Ankara, Islamabad, Tehran, Taipei, Tokyo — is reacting to the second one. That is why the reactions are structural (pacts, arsenals, deals over sea passages) rather than rhetorical.

So what would change the answer

Everything on this page hangs on one step: whether the US precision-munitions and interceptor stock is really as depleted as reported, and whether anything is refilling it. Watch that instead of twenty things. If it is true and unfixed, the Mecca pact hardens, the Iran–Oman arrangement becomes the Gulf's new law, Taiwan and Ukraine keep losing interception rate, Washington keeps paying in dollars because it cannot pay in ordnance, and Vietnam keeps paying the fuel bill. If it is refilling — a funded, multi-year production surge with allied coproduction, the direction the Pentagon's acquisition chief describes — most of the rest reverses in the same order it arrived.

The reversal is unusually cheap to watch, because unlike a ceasefire it requires no one's agreement. It requires an appropriation and a factory. Watch appropriations, not speeches — and note that the administration's incentive is to deny the shortfall right up until it is fixed.

The terms used above

Strait of Hormuz
The narrow sea passage at the mouth of the Gulf that a large share of the world's traded oil ships through. Its importance is geographic, not political: there is no quick alternative route for most of that volume.
